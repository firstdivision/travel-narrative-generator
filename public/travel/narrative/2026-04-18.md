I planned a lazy weekend and mostly stayed put
Then bought a mystery book and drank turmeric soot
The storm rolled in, the noodles hit late
Even the wasabi peas respected my plate

## Saturday April 18 to Sunday April 19 - Lazy Weekend, Loud Weather

I decided to be lazy this weekend. I won’t bore anyone, including myself, with the pedestrian things like laundry, cleaning bathrooms, and grocery shopping for basic supplies. Still, the day had its moments, and some of them were more than enough.

Breakfast at The Botanist Cafe was shakshuka, and it did exactly what shakshuka is supposed to do. It was simple, bold, and a little messy in the best way. It came in a wide, shallow bowl filled with a thick tomato base that had clearly been cooked down properly, rich and slightly sweet with just enough tang to keep it from turning dull. It wasn’t some delicate broth pretending to be breakfast. It had weight. I could see bits of pepper and seasoning worked through it, giving the sauce texture instead of smoothing everything into a polite red puree.

The eggs were the whole point, of course. Cracked directly into the sauce and cooked just until the whites had set while the yolks stayed soft, glossy, barely holding together. The kind of eggs that surrender the second your bread gets involved. On top there was a scattering of crumbled meat and herbs, enough to add a savory edge and make it feel like a full meal rather than just eggs floating in tomatoes. Off to the side were thick slices of toasted bread, golden and rough-cut, made for scooping rather than elegance. No finesse required. There was also a small salad, crisp lettuce with a light dressing, there mostly to cut through the richness and give me a brief reset between bites. It was hearty without being heavy, rustic without trying too hard, the kind of breakfast that leaned suspiciously close to lunch and got away with it.

After that I wandered around Tamarind Square a bit to see what else I could find. I came across a place selling teas and smoothies that I hadn’t seen open before, and since I had no schedule and nothing pressing to do, I took a seat and ordered a hot cleansing tea because I saw turmeric on the menu and I like turmeric.

It arrived in a French press with a simple glass, just hot, slightly cloudy amber-orange liquid that told me right away this was not standard tea. The flavor leaned earthy and a little bitter, with warmth behind it, probably ginger doing part of the lifting. There was a faint graininess too. Turmeric never really disappears into a drink. It lingers and settles and makes itself known. I could see it floating near the bottom of the French press when I poured more, as if challenging me to decide how committed I really was.

It wasn’t delicate or refined. It drank like something functional, something people had been making for a long time because it worked, not because it photographed well. Warming, a little drying on the tongue, and lingering. The kind of drink that asks to be taken slowly. Sitting outside with it, surrounded by green leaves inside the concrete box that is Tamarind Square, I could almost trick myself into thinking I was somewhere else for a while. That, and maybe the turmeric itself, put me at ease. I sat there taking small sips and reading the book I had brought with me, which felt like a perfectly respectable use of time.

Later I headed to the bookstore and found a section called “Blind Date With A Book,” which is a shelf of books that apparently do not wish to identify themselves. Each one was wrapped in plain brown paper and tied with string like a small packaged mystery. No cover art, no author name, none of the usual signals. Just a few typed clues on the front. “Dream girl.” “National tragedy.” “Survives a crash.” That was all you got.

It was called a blind date, which seemed fair. You weren’t choosing based on reputation or design or some glowing recommendation from the internet. You were going off instinct, a handful of clues, maybe a mood you couldn’t quite explain. There was something nice about how stripped down it was. No algorithm trying to anticipate my taste, no reviews to hide behind. Just me, a short description, and the possibility that I might either hate what I picked or end up liking it more because it arrived without ceremony.

I stood there longer than I expected, reading the same few lines over and over, trying to decide if they were enough.

Eventually, I just picked one.

Later, back in the apartment, the sound of an approaching thunderstorm caught my attention. I have always loved thunderstorms. My mother and I used to watch them from the window as they rolled through. The nighttime ones were always the best, though they also made staying awake feel like a personal challenge I was not built to win. We would sit together by the window under blankets while the pitch black outside kept getting replaced by flashes of lightning. Then we would count the seconds until the thunder came. Five seconds meant the storm was a mile away, ten meant two. We would count one flash, then the next, trying to figure out whether it was getting closer or farther away. It was a game, but a useful one, and tucked there by the window we were always quietly hoping the storm would get closer. Sleep always caught me in the end. My eyelids would get heavy in that unfair, unstoppable way, and no matter how hard I tried to stay awake, I never managed it.

It was late by the time I went looking for food. Not late dinner. More like nothing else is open, let’s see what survives this hour. The lights were half on, the kitchen half alive, and I wasn’t entirely sure what was happening behind the counter when I asked if they still had the Black Pepper Beef Noodles. There was a pause. A look. Maybe a nod. Good enough.

What arrived was a plate of black pepper beef noodles with no presentation tricks at all, just a dark glossy pile of food that smelled like it had no interest in being subtle. The noodles were thick and slightly chewy, coated in a sauce heavy on soy and cracked black pepper. You could see the pepper clinging to everything, those little black flecks promising heat of the slow-building kind, not chili heat but the kind that settles into your throat and stays there long enough to be noticed.

The beef was sliced thin and cooked hard enough to char at the edges, which is where the useful flavor lives anyway. Salty, smoky, a little caramelized. Mixed in were bits of cabbage and onion, softened down and soaked with sauce, adding just enough texture so it wasn’t all meat and noodles in a dark heap. It was oily in exactly the way a good stir-fry should be. Not greasy, just carrying the flavor properly and coating everything so each bite landed the same way. The first bite was immediate: pepper, salt, umami. No buildup, no subtle introduction. Just hot, savory, direct. Exactly what I wanted at that hour, whether I had planned to want it or not.

And whatever was going on behind that counter did not matter.

They made this. That was enough.

Back at home I still had the munchies and remembered I’d bought a big bag of wasabi peas, but not the usual open bag version that eventually becomes a shared buffet for me, the humidity, and whatever ant committee is operating in the room. These were individually wrapped, little sealed packets, each one looking oddly competent.

I tore one open and got the same experience every time: hard, glossy green peas under a thin crisp shell. The first bite gave that clean crunch, then the wasabi, or really green-dyed horseradish doing theater under an assumed name, came in fast. Not instantly brutal, but close enough. Sharp, nasal, sinus-clearing heat that vanished almost as quickly as it arrived, leaving behind salt and a faint sweetness from the pea itself.

The wrapping mattered more than I would have guessed. In a place where ants seem to operate like a coordinated search party, always probing and usually winning, this was one of the few snacks that actually held the line. No open bag abandoned on the counter. No overnight invasion. Just me opening one small packet at a time, on my own terms.

Simple, addictive, a little aggressive in flavor, but controlled.

And for once, I wasn’t sharing.
